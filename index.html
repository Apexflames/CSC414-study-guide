Introduction to HCI

What Human-Computer Interaction actually is, why it exists, and what "good usability" means in practice.

1.1 Why HCI exists — the CampusChef problem

Imagine a student builds an app called CampusChef to connect hungry students with other students cooking meals on campus. The idea is great. But the homepage is cluttered with five competing buttons, there's no obvious "Order Now," tapping "Find Food" opens a blank screen, and the back button freezes the app. Within five minutes, the user deletes it.

Nothing here is a coding bug — the servers work fine. The failure is in how the human experiences the system. That gap between "the software technically works" and "a person can actually use it without wanting to throw their phone" is exactly what HCI studies.

🪟 Formal Definition
Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) is the study, planning, and design of how people interact with computers — the interaction between end-users and computers. It is interdisciplinary, drawing on Computer Science, Psychology, Design, Cognitive Science, Ergonomics, Linguistics, and Artificial Intelligence.
🟣 Plain English
Think of HCI as the discipline that makes sure the "handshake" between a person and a machine doesn't feel awkward. It's the difference between a recipe that just lists ingredients and one that also tells you when to add them and what it should look like at each step.

1.2 Goals of HCI: Usability and UX

The primary goal of HCI is to improve usability and user experience (UX). These are related but not identical:

ConceptWhat it measures
UsabilityHow easy and effective it is for users to interact with a system — can they actually get things done?
User Experience (UX)Goes further than usability — how users feel while using the system: satisfaction, comfort, emotional response.
🟠 Real-Life Example
A food-ordering app can have perfect usability (every button works, checkout is fast) but poor UX if the confirmation screen is cold and confusing, leaving the user unsure whether the order actually went through.

1.3 Six qualities of good usability

QualityMeaningExample
Easy to learnFirst-time users figure it out quicklyA food app that guides new users step by step
Easy to rememberReturning users don't need to relearn itThe "Submit" button is still where you expect after a month away
EffectiveUsers accomplish their goals accuratelyBooking a flight without errors
EfficientTasks finish quickly, with minimal effortAutofill and shortcut keys
SafePrevents serious errors and allows recovery"Are you sure you want to delete?" prompts
EnjoyableThe experience feels pleasant, not stressfulFriendly copy, good visual feedback
🟠 Exam Tip
Examiners love asking you to distinguish Usability from UX, or to define HCI and list its interdisciplinary fields. Be ready to name at least four of: Computer Science, Psychology, Design, Cognitive Science, Ergonomics, Linguistics, AI.
2

The User Interface

What a UI is, how it engages human senses, and why it matters so much to a system's success.

2.1 What is a User Interface?

User interface design is a subset of HCI. The user interface (UI) is the part of a computer system and its software that users actually interact with. It works through human senses:

  • Sight — visual elements: text, icons, colour, layout.
  • Touch — physical interaction through mouse, keyboard, touchscreen, gestures.
  • Hearing — audio feedback, alerts, voice responses.
  • Speaking — modern systems also accept voice commands as input.
🪟 Formal Definition
A UI has two components: Input — how a person communicates their needs to the computer — and Output — how the computer conveys its results back to the user.

2.2 Why the interface matters so much

A well-designed UI isn't cosmetic — it directly drives the business:

  1. The user's window into the system — it's how people view and access everything the system can do.
  2. The medium for critical tasks — most business-critical operations happen through it.
  3. Shapes perception — a professional interface builds trust; a poor one makes a powerful system look unreliable.
  4. Affects efficiency & accuracy — confusing layouts cause mistakes and wasted time.
  5. Determines retention — bad interfaces drive users to competitors permanently.
  6. Affects emotion & stress — a clean interface lowers cognitive load and stress.

2.3 Benefits of good design

BenefitExplanationExample
ClarityEasy to read, interpret, understandGoogle's homepage — minimal, no distractions
SatisfactionReduces stress, increases positive emotionSpotify feels enjoyable to navigate
ProductivityTasks done faster, fewer errorsExcel's ribbon speeds up data entry
Lower costsLess training, fewer support callsNew staff pick up Canva with minimal training
Trust & retentionUsers return to systems that feel reliableAmazon's smooth checkout drives repeat purchases
🟣 Plain English
A UI is like the front desk of a hotel. The building (the backend code) might be five-star, but if the receptionist is confusing or rude, that's the entire experience the guest remembers.

History & Evolution of Interface Design

How interfaces moved from typed commands to voice, gesture, and multimodal experiences.

3.1 Command-Line Interfaces (CLIs)

The earliest interfaces were purely text-based. Users had to memorise exact commands and type them precisely, with no visual indication of what was being deleted or whether it could be recovered — screens were often cluttered with cryptic symbols and poor labelling. Examples: MS-DOS, the Unix shell.

🟠 Real-Life Example
Deleting a file with rm -rf in Unix gives no visual confirmation and no undo — compare that to dragging a file into a trash bin.

3.2 Direct manipulation through GUIs (1980s)

Graphical User Interfaces emerged with the Xerox Star, Apple Lisa, and later the Macintosh, introducing windows, icons, menus, and pointers (WIMP). Benefits: reduced memory load (no commands to remember), immediate visual feedback, and easier learning for non-technical users.

3.3 Web-based interfaces (mid-1990s–present)

The rise of the internet created a need for browser-based interaction. Web UIs became the default for email, shopping, and search, built on HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. Design emphasis shifted from desktop metaphors toward hyperlinks, forms, scrollable content, and responsive layouts.

3.4 Mobile & touch interfaces (2007–present)

Smartphones and tablets replaced mouse actions with touch gestures — tap, swipe, pinch — and pushed design toward minimalism, flat design, and responsiveness.

3.5 Modern interfaces (2015 onward)

Voice interfaces (Siri, Alexa), Augmented and Virtual Reality, conversational chatbots, and multimodal interfaces (voice + touch + vision) working together.

🟠 Exam Tip
A classic question is "trace the evolution of UI design" — you need the five eras in order with at least one defining feature and one example each. The Play button above literally rehearses this answer for you.

Graphical User Interface (GUI) & the WIMP Model

How GUIs use visual metaphors, and the four building blocks — Windows, Icons, Menus, Pointer — that structure them.

4.1 What is a GUI?

A Graphical User Interface allows users to interact with digital devices using graphical elements — windows, icons, buttons, menus — instead of relying solely on text commands. GUI is the foundation of modern interface design: nearly every interface since has built on it. Its purpose is to make interaction more intuitive and visual, reduce the need to memorise commands, and present functionality through visual metaphors.

🪟 Formal Definition
A visual metaphor is a design technique where real-world objects or concepts are represented visually in a digital interface — using familiar images or symbols to represent digital actions or features — so the interface is more intuitive and easier to learn (e.g. trash bin, folders, printer icon, scissors icon, battery bar).

4.2 Three types of visual metaphor

TypeWhat it doesExample
Object-basedUses real-world physical objectsFolder, Trash bin, Desktop
Action-basedRepresents behaviours or operationsDrag-and-drop mimics physically moving a file
EnvironmentalSimulates a physical space or contextThe desktop metaphor — icons arranged like items on a real desk

4.4 Visual presentation & feedback

GUI design relies on colour, contrast, fonts, alignment, spacing, and visual hierarchy. Components like buttons, sliders, drop-downs, and tabs help users act. Feedback is essential — a button should visibly change when hovered or clicked, so the user knows something happened.

🟠 Real-Life Example
Clicking "Submit" changes the button's colour to show the form is processing — without that, users often click it five more times.

GUI vs. Web UI

Comparing desktop-style graphical interfaces with browser-based interfaces, and the extra challenges the web introduces.

5.1 Key differences

FeatureGUI (Desktop)Web UI (Browser)
PlatformLocal applications, e.g. PhotoshopAccessed via browser, e.g. Google Docs
PerformanceFast, uses system resourcesDepends on internet speed and server response
Interaction styleTool-focused, deep menus and controlsNavigation-focused, hyperlink-driven
CustomisationCan be personalised for the userLimited by HTML/CSS and browser constraints
ExamplesMS Excel, PhotoshopAmazon, Gmail, Coursera
Real-life analogyA fully-equipped home kitchenA well-run restaurant — less customisable, more accessible

In short: GUI is typically more feature-rich and suited to complex tasks, while Web UI is more accessible and suited to content interaction or services.

5.2 Growth & importance of Web UI

With the rise of the internet, most user interaction is now web-based. Web UIs must support desktops, tablets, and smartphones alike — good design ensures accessibility, responsiveness, and efficiency.

🟠 Real-Life Example
A travel site like Booking.com must let users filter, compare, and book rooms cleanly on any device.

5.3 Challenges of Web UI

  • Device variability — different screen sizes, touch vs mouse.
  • Slow networks — loading time directly affects usability.
  • Security & privacy — authentication, cookies.
  • Inconsistent behaviour — a site may look fine on Chrome but break on an older browser due to browser-specific features.

Principles of User Interface Design

The guiding rules that make interfaces usable and consistent — general principles, the Xerox Star principles, and Shneiderman's Eight Golden Rules.

7.1 Why design principles are needed

Design principles are guidelines that help designers build systems that are usable and consistent. They exist to:

  • Guide consistency — buttons, menus, and icons behave the same way everywhere.
  • Reduce user errors — disabling unavailable options, clear error messages, confirming destructive actions.
  • Improve learnability & efficiency — clear instructions, recognisable icons, logical grouping.
  • Provide a framework for evaluation — checkpoints during prototyping, testing, and critique.
  • Support user-centred design — keeping focus on user needs, accessibility, and context of use.

7.2 General principles

PrincipleExplanationExample
AestheticsGood visual layout, colour, spacingClean spacing in Apple interfaces
ClarityAvoid ambiguity in functions and labelsUse "Save" instead of "Record"
ConsistencyUniform design across screensSame button layout on every page
ControlUsers feel in charge, not forcedAllow users to cancel an upload
CompatibilitySuits users' devices, culture, expectationsRight-to-left text for Arabic users
FlexibilityMultiple ways to do the same taskCopy via right-click or Ctrl+C
TransparencyHide technical detail, focus on contentAuto-updates without showing complex logs
RecoveryAllow undo and error correctionUndo in Gmail, recycle bin for deleted files

7.3 Xerox Star design principles

The Xerox Star was the first commercial GUI system, and its design team pioneered principles that still shape GUIs today.

PrincipleExplanationWhere it shows up
Visible, manipulable objectsUsers interact with icons and controls directlyDrag-and-drop files, toolbars
Uniformity & consistencyActions behave similarly system-wideSame button styles and shortcuts everywhere
WYSIWYGScreen reflects the final output accuratelyWord processor print preview matches the printed page
Visual orderLayout, contrast, and grouping guide attentionPanels, cards, tabs arranged logically
Feedback & dialogueClear system response to every actionProgress bars, status messages, error alerts

7.4 Shneiderman's Eight Golden Rules

A widely tested checklist for evaluating interaction design.

  1. Strive for consistency — uniform behaviour throughout the system.
  2. Enable frequent users to use shortcuts — accelerators like Ctrl+S improve speed.
  3. Offer informative feedback — visual or audio response to every action.
  4. Design dialogs to yield closure — let users know a task is complete, e.g. "Upload complete!"
  5. Prevent errors — disable unavailable options, confirm destructive actions.
  6. Permit easy reversal of actions — undo and redo buttons.
  7. Support internal locus of control — users should feel in control of actions, not the system.
  8. Reduce short-term memory load — use dropdowns and visible options instead of requiring memorisation.
🟠 Exam Tip
"List and explain Shneiderman's Eight Golden Rules" is a near-guaranteed question. Learn them in order — the numbering itself is often part of the marking scheme.

GUI Toolkits

The pre-built libraries developers use to construct interfaces instead of drawing every pixel by hand.

8.1 What is a GUI toolkit?

A GUI toolkit is a collection of pre-built libraries or frameworks developers use to create visual interfaces — buttons, windows, sliders, menus, dialog boxes — without writing low-level drawing code for every element. Toolkits abstract that complexity into high-level building blocks.

ComponentDescription
Widgets/ControlsGUI elements like buttons, checkboxes, text fields, menus
Layout managersControl how elements are arranged (grid, box, stack)
Event handlingDetect and respond to clicks, drags, keypresses
Rendering engineDraws the interface elements on screen
Themes & stylingCustomise colours, fonts, sizes
Platform abstractionLets an app look and behave the same across OSes

8.2 Popular GUI toolkits

Tkinter (Python) — built-in with Python, simple, cross-platform. Use case: academic tools, internal apps, beginner projects.

Qt / PyQt / PySide (C++, Python) — professional-grade, WYSIWYG design with Qt Designer, rich widgets, supports animation and multimedia. Use case: desktop apps, commercial and scientific software.

GTK (C, Python via PyGObject) — used in GNOME-based Linux environments, high-performance native widgets. Use case: Linux-native, open-source projects.

JavaFX / Swing (Java) — Swing is the older, mature toolkit; JavaFX is its modern replacement with 2D/3D and CSS styling. Use case: cross-platform enterprise apps, teaching tools.

Kivy (Python) — designed for touch and mobile-first apps, lightweight, supports Android and iOS. Use case: mobile apps, touchscreens, Raspberry Pi.

Electron (JS, HTML, CSS) — builds desktop apps with web technologies (Node.js backend, HTML/CSS frontend). Use case: apps like VS Code, Slack, Discord.

8.3 Toolkit comparison

ToolkitLanguageBest forProsCons
TkinterPythonSimplicityEasy, built-inLimited widgets

8.4 GUI toolkits & HCI principles

Toolkits are fundamental to HCI because they translate abstract user needs into tangible interactions, enforce usability heuristics through consistent components, and provide the tools for feedback, visibility, and affordance — plus rapid prototyping before full deployment.

🔴 Key Insight
Consistency, feedback, and error prevention — three HCI principles from Topic 7 — are realised concretely through toolkits: widgets behave the same across screens, buttons give visual feedback automatically, and toolkits can restrict input formats to prevent bad data entirely.
9

Design Evaluation in HCI

How designers systematically check whether an interface actually works for real users — and a full worked case study.

9.1 What is design evaluation, and why do it?

Design evaluation is the systematic assessment of an interface to determine how well it supports users, whether it meets usability standards, and what needs improving before or after deployment. It's done to catch usability problems early, measure satisfaction, ensure accessibility, validate design decisions with evidence, and save cost by reducing future rework.

9.2 Formative vs. Summative Evaluation

TypeWhenGoalExample
FormativeDuring the design processImprove the design as it evolvesTesting wireframes or paper prototypes with users
SummativeAfter development / at the final stageAssess how the final product performsMeasuring task completion in the finished app
🟣 Plain English
Formative evaluation is a chef tasting the soup while it's still cooking and adjusting the seasoning. Summative evaluation is the food critic tasting the finished dish and giving it a score.

9.3 Evaluation methods

Methods split into two families: Empirical (real users interact with the system) and Analytical (experts assess it without users).

FamilyMethodWhat it involves
A. Empirical (user-based)Usability testingUsers perform predefined tasks while observers record time, errors, satisfaction
Field studiesObserving users in their natural environment
Interviews & questionnairesGathering subjective feedback, preferences, pain points
A/B testingUsers try two design versions; the better-performing one is selected
B. Analytical (expert-based)Heuristic evaluationExperts assess the interface against usability heuristics (e.g. Nielsen's 10 principles)
Cognitive walkthroughExperts simulate a new user's step-by-step interaction to spot confusion points

9.4 Usability metrics

MetricDescription
EffectivenessCan users complete tasks correctly?
EfficiencyHow quickly can users complete tasks?
SatisfactionHow pleasant is the experience?
Error rateHow often do users make mistakes?
LearnabilityHow easy is it to learn the system?
RetentionCan users still use it correctly after time away?

9.5 Worked case study: the AAUA Student Portal

Adekunle Ajasin University (AAUA) built a new student portal for course registration and tuition payment. Before launch, the HCI team ran a full evaluation combining Formative + Summative and Empirical + Analytical methods. Step through what actually happened:

Live Demo
AAUA Student Portal: Evaluation Walkthrough
Watch task completion, time-on-task, satisfaction and errors move from "before" to "after" as the redesign happens.

Formative — paper prototype, 10 students: testers couldn't easily find the "Register Course" button, navigation between sections lacked clarity, and unlabelled icons were unclear. This directly informed a redesign of layout, button placement, and visual labels.

Usability testing (empirical), 12 students: some clicked wrong buttons due to unclear labels, "back to homepage" confused many, and some forms gave no confirmation after submission. Impact: labels redesigned, feedback added, menus reorganised.

Field studies: in hostels/labs, students didn't scroll far enough to find action buttons, poor network caused repeated button presses, and screen overload made users ignore instructions. Impact: sticky buttons, loading animations, visual cues added.

Interviews & questionnaires: students liked the colours and layout but often "got lost" during registration and asked for step-by-step instructions. Impact: tooltips, page hints, and short instructions were added.

A/B testing: "icons only" vs "icons with labels" — labelled icons won on both speed and user confidence, so labels were made permanent across the interface.

Heuristic evaluation (analytical), 3 experts, Nielsen's 10 heuristics: found no loading feedback (visibility of system status), confusing "Programme Fees" wording (match with real world), inconsistent buttons (consistency), too many hidden functions (recognition over recall), and no help available. Directly guided feedback messages, consistent icon styles, and renamed terms.

Cognitive walkthrough: experts simulated registration and result-checking, asking "will the user know what to do here?" — found the registration workflow had too many unclear steps and that "Add Course" wasn't visible enough. Simplified steps and highlighted call-to-action buttons followed.

Summative — revised portal, 10 new students: confirmed the fixes worked (see metrics in the demo above).

Issue identifiedImprovement implemented
"Programme Fees" unclearRenamed to "School Fees"
No feedback after submitting formsAdded confirmation messages and loading spinners
Icons lacked meaningAdded text labels beside all icons
Deep menus, confusing layoutSimplified navigation, reduced options per page
No guidance for first-time usersAdded tooltips, page hints, short instructions
No visible progress during registrationIntroduced a progress bar for multi-step processes

9.6 Challenges & best practices

ChallengesBest practices
Recruiting users representative of the target audienceEvaluate early and often in the design process
Bias in self-reported feedbackCombine multiple methods, e.g. usability testing + heuristic evaluation
Limited resources (time, cost, tools)Document findings and act on them
Misinterpretation of resultsUse realistic tasks and representative users, and focus on user goals and context

Likely · 2025/2026

1. Define Human-Computer Interaction and explain why it is described as interdisciplinary.

HCI is the study, planning, and design of how people interact with computers — the interaction between end-users and computer systems. It is interdisciplinary because no single field can address all the factors that make a system usable: Computer Science explains how the software and hardware work; Psychology explains how humans think, perceive, and remember; Design contributes attractive, usable layouts; Cognitive Science covers mental processes like attention; Ergonomics covers physical comfort and safety; Linguistics informs natural-language interfaces; and Artificial Intelligence powers intelligent software behaviour. Each field solves a different piece of the "can a human actually use this" puzzle.

📍 See Topic 1.1
Likely · 2025/2026

2. Distinguish between usability and user experience (UX), giving an example of each.

Usability measures whether users can interact with a system easily and effectively — can they actually get the task done? UX goes further, covering how the user feels while doing it: satisfaction, comfort, and emotional response. A banking app might have excellent usability (fast, error-free transfers) yet poor UX if its tone feels cold or its confirmation screens leave the user anxious about whether the transfer worked. Good design aims for both.

📍 See Topic 1.2
Likely · 2025/2026

3. Trace the evolution of user interface design from command-line interfaces to modern interfaces, giving one defining feature of each era.

Interfaces evolved through five broad eras. CLIs (e.g. MS-DOS, Unix shell) required users to memorise and type exact text commands, with no visual feedback. GUIs (1980s, e.g. Xerox Star, Apple Lisa, Macintosh) introduced the WIMP model — windows, icons, menus, pointers — cutting memory load through direct manipulation. Web UIs (mid-1990s onward) moved interaction into the browser using HTML/CSS/JavaScript, built around hyperlinks and forms. Mobile/touch interfaces (2007 onward) replaced mouse actions with tap, swipe, and pinch gestures, favouring minimal, flat, responsive design. Modern interfaces (2015 onward) add voice assistants, AR/VR, chatbots, and multimodal input that combines voice, touch, and vision.

📍 See Topic 3
Likely · 2025/2026

4. Explain the WIMP model and describe each of its four components with an example.

WIMP — Windows, Icons, Menus, Pointer — is the architectural blueprint for how users interact with GUI systems. Windows are independent viewing areas for apps or content, e.g. two Word documents open side by side. Icons are small visual symbols representing files, tools, or actions, e.g. a trash can icon for deletion. Menus are organised lists of commands, e.g. File → Save As. The Pointer is the movable on-screen cursor used to interact with objects, typically controlled by a mouse, trackpad, or finger. Together these four elements let users control a system visually and interactively rather than through memorised text commands.

Likely · 2025/2026

5. Compare GUI (desktop) and Web UI across at least three features.

GUI applications run locally (e.g. Photoshop) and are fast because they use system resources directly, whereas Web UIs are accessed through a browser (e.g. Google Docs) and their performance depends on internet speed and server response. GUIs tend to be tool-focused with deep menus suited to complex tasks, while Web UIs are navigation-focused and hyperlink-driven, better suited to content and services. GUIs can also be personalised more deeply for the user, while Web UIs are constrained by what HTML/CSS and the browser allow.

📍 See Topic 5.1
Likely · 2025/2026

6. State and explain Shneiderman's Eight Golden Rules of interface design.

(1) Strive for consistency in actions and terminology. (2) Enable frequent users to use shortcuts, like Ctrl+S, to work faster. (3) Offer informative feedback for every user action. (4) Design dialogs to yield closure, e.g. an "Upload complete!" message so users know a task finished. (5) Prevent errors by disabling invalid options and confirming destructive actions. (6) Permit easy reversal of actions through undo/redo. (7) Support internal locus of control so users feel they are directing the system, not the other way round. (8) Reduce short-term memory load by showing visible options instead of requiring users to remember commands.

7. A university is about to deploy a new mobile app for hospital appointment booking. Using the AAUA Student Portal case study as a template, explain how you would carry out a full design evaluation before launch.

Following the same structure used for the AAUA portal, evaluation would combine Formative and Summative stages using both Empirical and Analytical methods. In the formative stage, a paper or clickable prototype of the appointment-booking flow would be tested with a small group of patients performing tasks such as booking and cancelling an appointment, recording task success, time taken, and points of confusion — these findings drive an early redesign. Empirical methods would follow: usability testing with a larger group to catch remaining friction, field studies observing patients booking appointments in real settings such as a waiting room with unreliable Wi-Fi, interviews/questionnaires to capture subjective satisfaction, and A/B testing to compare, for example, a calendar-view booking screen against a list-view one. In parallel, analytical methods bring in experts: a heuristic evaluation against Nielsen's 10 principles to catch violations like missing loading feedback or inconsistent buttons, and a cognitive walkthrough simulating a first-time patient's steps to flag likely confusion points. Finally, a summative evaluation on the revised app — measuring task completion rate, time per task, error frequency, and satisfaction against the original baseline — confirms whether the redesign actually solved the problems found earlier, the same way AAUA's task completion rose from 70% to 92% after its redesign.

8. Explain the three types of visual metaphor used in GUI design, with an example of each.

Object-based metaphors use real-world physical objects, such as a folder or trash bin icon. Action-based metaphors represent behaviours or operations, such as drag-and-drop mimicking the physical act of moving a file. Environmental metaphors simulate a physical space or context, such as the desktop metaphor, where icons and files are arranged like items on a real desk. Visual metaphors matter because they let users apply knowledge from the physical world to a digital system, reducing the need to memorise abstract commands.
